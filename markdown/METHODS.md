## 1. The question

TESS observes most of the sky in ~27 day blocks separated by months or years.
A planet with a period longer than a sector's span cannot show the ≥3 evenly
spaced transits that a standard box-least-squares search needs. It instead
produces:

- **no transit** — every conjunction fell in a gap,
- **a mono-transit** — exactly one transit caught, period unconstrained,
- **an ambiguous duo** — two or more transits caught, but several periods fit,
- **a solved system** — exactly one period survives.

Three research goals follow:

1. **Strategy evaluation.** Given a proposed future TESS pointing schedule, how
   many otherwise-unusable planets does it convert into usable ones?
2. **Revisit staggering — the operational deliverable.** *How should returns to
   a given part of the sky be spaced in time* so as to minimise the number of
   surviving aliases, as a function of period range? This is the recommendation
   the paper exists to make. It is a sharper question than goal 1: not merely
   *which* fields to revisit, but *when*, since the alias ladder `dt/n` is
   generated by the gap `dt` itself. Fewer surviving aliases means less
   follow-up needed to resolve a system, so the objective is denominated in
   downstream telescope time, not in a count of upgrades.
3. **Completeness.** What fraction of long-period transiting planets does TESS
   recover, as a function of period and sky position?

All three are answered by injection and recovery against the *real* observing
windows, so the fidelity of the window table is the thing that matters most.

### Scope: this is one factor, deliberately

A true duo-transit yield requires stellar parameters, population-level planet
parameter distributions, photometric precision, detectability, vetting
efficiency, and more. Each of those is a research programme. Attempting all of
them at once means none of them can be examined carefully, and the
schedule-dependent effect — the only one the mission can actually *choose* —
gets buried under nuisance parameters it has nothing to do with.

So this project deliberately factorises:

```
yield  =  window function  ×  detectability  ×  underlying astrophysics
```

and computes **only the first term**, exactly and without approximation, as a
function of period, sky position, and observing schedule. Ignoring stellar
parameters and transit depths is not an accepted limitation to be apologised
for; it is what makes it possible to isolate the strategy trade-off and drill
into it properly.

This has two consequences worth stating up front in the paper:

- **The result is reusable.** Anyone building a yield forecast can take this
  window function as their first term and supply their own detectability model
  and occurrence rates. A cleanly separated, schedule-aware first factor is more
  useful to the community than another end-to-end yield number entangled with
  one particular set of stellar and planetary assumptions.
- **The simplifications are the design, not the shortfall.** Nothing here is
  waiting on an SNR model to become valid. Folding in detectability would answer
  a *different* question, and would do so at the cost of the one being asked.

### Prior art and how this project relates to it

Three literatures exist and do not intersect (see `LITERATURE_REVIEW.md` §3):

1. **Real-window TESS completeness** — Rodel et al. 2024 (TIaRA, MNRAS 529, 715)
   and Cooke et al. 2019 (A&A 631, A83) both build true per-window functions from
   real timestamps, but ask *how many planets?*, not *which period states?*
2. **Alias resolution** — Cooke et al. 2021 (MNRAS 500, 5088), Osborn's
   `MonoTools` (ascl:2204.020), Becker et al. 2019 (HIP 41378). These enumerate
   and kill aliases, but take the observing schedule as **given** and optimise
   *follow-up*.
3. **Pointing-strategy comparison** — Bouma et al. 2017 (arXiv:1705.08891),
   Kunimoto et al. 2022 (AJ 163, 290). These treat the schedule as the free
   variable, but score it by *total planet yield*, which is dominated by short
   periods and is therefore nearly blind to the effect studied here.

**This work is the intersection: scoring candidate TESS schedules by their
effect on the alias state of long-period systems** — and, per goal 2, inverting
that into a recommendation about revisit spacing.

Note what the factorisation in the previous subsection buys against literature 1.
Rodel et al. 2024 and Cooke et al. 2019 build genuine window functions but
immediately multiply them by a detection model, so the window-function factor is
never exposed as a reusable object. Extracting it and mapping it across schedule
space is a different and complementary contribution, not a lesser version of
theirs.

**Cooke et al. 2021 is the closest prior art and the method's origin.** Both of
this project's rule-out criteria (§5) are published there, in their words:

> "For each alias, we create a list of all times at which the system would
> transit ... and check if there are any TESS observations within half a transit
> duration of any of these."

> "If no transit is seen it shows that the true period is not one of those that
> predicted a transit this night and we therefore remove all periods that
> predicted a transit that night from our list of aliases."

The alias ladder is theirs and the introduction should say so plainly. What is
added here is (i) the whole real 8-year baseline rather than a Year-1/Year-3
pair, (ii) the four-state outcome algebra applied to *every* sky position rather
than to a detected candidate list, and (iii) the pointing schedule as the
decision variable. That an established method is being applied to a new variable
is a normal and good position to be in — the framework being settled is what
makes it usable as a measurement instrument for schedules.

Cooke et al. 2019 also independently corroborates this project's central
empirical finding. Of 339 predicted Year-1 southern monotransits, ~80% (266)
transit again during a Year-4 re-observation — but **75% of those (189) show
only a single transit in Year 4**, i.e. they become duotransits with a live
alias ladder, not solved systems. They encountered that while pursuing a yield
and treated it as an obstacle; here it is the signal — a direct measurement of
how a revisit gap redistributes systems across alias states, which is precisely
what goal 2 needs. The finding that upgrades out of `MONO` and `NO_TRANSIT`
outweigh the classic `AMBIGUOUS → SOLVED` channel (D12) is the same structural
effect seen from the other side, and the agreement is worth stating explicitly:
an independent group hit it with different code on a different baseline.

## 3. The window table

Everything downstream consumes one abstraction, a table with columns:

```
sector | pointing | t_start | t_end | predicted
```

`pointing` is the key idea: it names *which sector's sky footprint* applies,
while `sector` and the times say *when* the observing happens. For the real
mission the two are equal. For a hypothetical future schedule they differ —
slot 107 can be pointed at sector 1's field. This is what lets a candidate
strategy be expressed as data rather than code.

`predicted` marks synthesized windows so real and forecast coverage can always
be separated in a published number.

### Sector geometry is not uniform

Sectors are **not** 27 days centred on a midtime. Measured over sectors 1–106:
spans run **24.3–57.4 d** (median 26.5), and segment counts run **2–8** per
sector. Since sector 56 many sectors have four orbit segments rather than two;
sectors 97 and 98 are genuine ~55 d double-length pointings with eight
segments. Any approximation of the form `midtime ± 13.5 d` is wrong, and was
wrong in the original notebook code.

### Synthesizing future windows

`mission.synthesize_windows` extrapolates new sectors by inheriting the
**median of the last 20 measured sectors**: cadence, span, segment count and
inter-segment gap. It anchors off the *end* of the measured schedule rather
than the last sector start, so an unusually long final sector cannot overlap
the first synthesized one.

Consequence worth stating in the paper: **all candidate strategies share
identical timing.** The only thing that varies between them is the `pointing`
column. Differences in score are therefore attributable purely to sky
selection, not to cadence.

> **This is a clean control, and it is also currently a blocker for goal 2.**
> Holding timing fixed isolates sky selection perfectly — but revisit *staggering*
> is a question about timing, so no experiment in the current design can answer
> it. The five strategies differ in *where* they look, never in *when* they
> return. To make a recommendation about spacing, the schedule generator has to
> vary the revisit gap `dt` at fixed sky selection — the transpose of what it
> does now. See §10; the machinery supports it (a strategy is just a window
> table) but no such strategy has been defined.

## 4. Injection

For each grid point (period `P`, phase `φ`, sky position):

- **Ephemeris.** `tc = t_start + φ·P`, transits every `P` across the baseline.
  Phase is sampled uniformly on `[0, 1)`, which is the correct prior for an
  unknown conjunction time.
- **Duration.** Central transit of a solar-density host,
  `T = 13 hr · (P/365 d)^(1/3)`. Long-period planets have proportionally longer
  transits, which matters for whether an event fits inside a window. This is the
  **`b = 0`, `e = 0` limit of the Winn (2014) formulation** used by Rodel et al.
  2024, so it is an *upper bound* on duration — and, because a longer event is
  harder to fit entirely inside a window, a *lower* bound on recoverability at
  fixed geometry. State the convention; it breaks comparability if left silent.
- **Detection criterion.** A transit counts as observed only if the **entire**
  event, `tc ± T/2`, falls inside a single window. Events straddling a downlink
  gap or a sector boundary are correctly excluded.

**Deliberately excluded:** signal-to-noise, transit depth, stellar variability,
systematics, impact parameter, eccentricity. Every transit that geometrically
fits inside a window is assumed detected. Per §1, this is the *point* — these
are the later-in-the-process factors that the observing schedule has no bearing
on, and holding them out is what leaves the schedule dependence visible.

The resulting quantity is a pure **window function**: the first factor of
`yield = window function × detectability × astrophysics`, computed exactly. It
is a *necessary condition* for recovery — a planet failing it cannot be
recovered by any amount of photometric precision — and it is deliberately not a
yield.

Two things follow for how numbers get reported:

- **Never quote an outcome fraction as a yield.** Rodel et al. 2024 measure
  giant-planet completeness falling from >80% at `P < 6.25` d to **<10% at
  200–400 d**, so the second factor is both small and strongly period-dependent
  in exactly the regime studied here. An unqualified "28.9% solved" will be
  misread.
- **Report in a form others can multiply.** Because the intended use is as a
  reusable first factor, results should be published resolved by period and sky
  position — not only as marginalised headline percentages — so that a reader
  supplying their own detectability model and occurrence rates can combine
  against it directly. A single all-sky number cannot be used that way.

### Sky sampling

Targets are collapsed onto **distinct sector combinations**
(`targets.unique_sector_sets`), carrying a `weight` = number of catalog stars
sharing that combination. At sectors 1–106 there are **2,200 distinct
combinations** covering the 7,446 multi-sector targets. This is the main
efficiency win — the calculation runs once per combination, not once per star —
and the weights are what make a sky-averaged completeness meaningful.

Runs use `min_sectors=2`, because single-sector targets have a closed-form
window function (`targets.completion_for_single_sectors`) and need no
simulation.

## 5. Classification: the alias framework

This is the scientific core.

Two transits separated by a gap `dt` are consistent with **any** period `dt/n`
for integer `n`. `aliases.find_all_aliases` builds the ladder from the
**smallest** gap between consecutive observed transits, enumerating `dt/n` down
to a floor of `min_period = 13 d`. Cooke et al. 2021 use **~10 d**, justified
differently — "only one transit is seen in each TESS observing run" rather than
"BLS would have found it". The two conventions give different alias counts and
therefore non-comparable ambiguous fractions, so the floor must be declared
whenever a number is quoted against theirs. See D7 and its 2026-08-21 amendment.

An alias is **ruled out** if either:

1. it predicts a detectable transit inside a window that came up **empty**, or
2. it fails to reproduce a transit that **was** observed (tolerance
   `PERIOD_MATCH_TOL = 0.1 d`).

**Both criteria are Cooke et al. 2021's**, quoted in §1. Cite them as the origin
of the rule-out method. For scale, they report an average of **38 aliases per
duotransit system**, and resolved 207/395 (~52%) with 50 d of NGTS photometry
plus CORALIE spectroscopy.

Outcome flags: `NO_TRANSIT = 0`, `MONO = 1`, `AMBIGUOUS = 2`, `SOLVED = 3`,
`ERROR = -100`. The ordering is meaningful — higher is strictly better — and
several checks rely on it.

> **Invariant.** The true injected period must *never* be ruled out. It is the
> primary correctness check on the whole pipeline and is enforced in
> `tests/test_core.py` over hundreds of random real-sky systems. An `ERROR`
> flag means the transit grid and the windows disagree and is always a bug.
>
> Note what this invariant does *not* test: it exercises the alias logic only,
> and is self-referential — it says nothing about whether the window table is
> right. That needs external checks; see §7.

### Terminology and comparability

The literature's vocabulary, adopted here (`LITERATURE_REVIEW.md` §2):
**monotransit** (not "single-transit candidate"), **duotransit**, **biennial
duotransit** (Rodel et al. 2024's term for the specific Year-1 + Year-3 case),
**period alias**, and **period-resolved** for what the code calls `SOLVED`.

The four-state *ordered outcome* scheme is not standard. The literature uses
mono/duo as categories of **candidate**, not as outcome states of a survey — the
difference is a genuine contribution, but it obliges the paper to define the
states early and give the mapping explicitly:

| This work | Literature |
|---|---|
| `MONO` | monotransit |
| `AMBIGUOUS` | duotransit — **but see the caveat below** |
| `SOLVED` | period-resolved |
| `NO_TRANSIT` | **no counterpart** — existing studies condition on detection |

> **`AMBIGUOUS` is a superset of "duotransit."** It admits systems with three or
> more observed transits that still have surviving aliases. The 20.3% ambiguous
> fraction is therefore **not** directly comparable to Hawthorn et al. 2024's 85
> duotransit candidates without stating the relation, and any comparison against
> a published duo count must first restrict to `n_observed == 2`. See D14.

## 6. Scoring a candidate strategy

`experiment.score_strategy(results, windows, new_windows, sector_sets)`
concatenates the baseline and proposed windows and **re-runs the full analysis**
on the identical grid, with the same per-period durations. Because the start
time is unchanged, every injected ephemeris is bit-identical to the baseline;
only coverage grows.

Output columns: `flag_new`, `n_observed_new`, `n_surviving_new`,
`surviving_new`, plus `new_transits`, `upgraded`, `resolved` and a readable
`transition` label. `experiment.transition_matrix` renders the before→after
table.

`experiment.touched_sets` skips sky positions the strategy never revisits —
their outcomes provably cannot change — which holds the cost to ~0.5× a
baseline grid pass per strategy.

**Why re-analysis rather than incremental pruning** (see decision D12): pruning
a stored alias ladder can only *remove* candidate periods, so it can never
promote a mono or a miss. Since mono + missed are ~51% of the baseline
population against ambiguous's ~20%, that structural limit hid most of what an
extended mission actually delivers.

## 7. Validation and known data defects

- `mission.validate_sector_alignment()` compares each sector's midtime in the
  orbit table against `tess_stars2px`'s pointing midtimes and warns on
  disagreement. **Run it after every orbit-table refresh.** MIT renumbers
  sectors when the schedule changes — the Oct-2025 table labelled the
  2025-10-12 orbits "98", the Aug-2026 table labels them "97" — and because
  sector number is the only join key, a renumbering silently repoints an
  existing analysis. Renumbering shows up as tens of days; ~7 d is ordinary
  schedule drift against the pointings frozen into tess-point.
- `mission.last_complete_sector()` derives the last usable sector rather than
  hardcoding it, excluding sectors still observing whose segment lists are
  truncated. Currently 106.
- **`mission.ORBIT_TABLE_ERRATA`** corrects a genuine bug in MIT's 2026-08-20
  file: orbit 215 appears four times under sector 102 (the only orbit in 329
  rows appearing more than twice), two of those rows span 12.50/12.13 d (the
  only segments >10 d in the four-segment era, mean 6.64 d), and orbits 216–217
  are absent. Reading the rows in time order as 215, 216 (S102) and 217 split
  in two (S103) restores contiguous numbering and reproduces the official
  boundaries from MIT's own pointing table and HEASARC (102: 03/27–04/21,
  103: 04/21–05/17), dropping midtime offsets from +7.12/+7.41 d to
  +0.44/+0.70 d. There was no spacecraft anomaly — consecutive blocks are
  separated by the normal 4–5 h downlink gap throughout. The erratum applies
  only while the defect signature still matches, so it no-ops after MIT
  republishes. Reported to `tesshelp@bigbang.gsfc.nasa.gov`.

Test suite: **27 tests**, covering ephemeris construction, the
full-transit-in-window rule, alias enumeration and rule-out, the
true-period-survives invariant, orbit-table/pointing agreement, the errata, and
strategy scoring (including that no outcome may ever degrade).

### External checks — identified, not yet done

Every validation above is **internal**. The true-period invariant tests the alias
logic against itself and can pass with a badly wrong window table. Three
external checks were identified in the literature review; each costs roughly one
figure and none has been performed yet.

1. **Bound Rodel et al. 2024 from above.** TIaRA is the SNR-aware version of this
   calculation over Years 1 and 3. A pure-geometry window function must exceed
   their completeness everywhere. If it does not, something here is wrong. Their
   headline numbers for comparison: 403(+64/−38) detections at `P > 25` d,
   215(+37/−23) monotransits, 113(+22/−13) biennial duotransits, 110 at
   `P > 100` d.
2. **Consistency with Hawthorn et al. 2024's 85 observed duotransits** (Cycles 1
   and 3, southern ecliptic hemisphere, 8 < Tmag < 14). This is the *observed*
   duo population. Requires the `n_observed == 2` restriction noted in §5, and
   folding in a detection efficiency, so it is an order-of-magnitude check
   rather than a precise one — but predicting far more or fewer than 85 over
   that footprint would be a calibration problem worth explaining.
3. **Reproduce Villanueva et al. 2019's analytic result for one sector.** They
   integrate `P(exactly 1 transit)` and `P(≥2 transits)` over a finite baseline
   in closed form. That is the analytic limit of this simulation for a single
   idealised window, and — unlike the internal invariant — it tests the **window
   logic** rather than the alias logic. Cheap, and it belongs in an appendix.

A fourth, softer check: `MonoTools` already folds a window function into its
alias prior. Reading `github.com/hposborn/MonoTools` to see how coarse that
treatment is would establish whether this work's population-level window
function is a concrete improvement on what alias-probability tools currently
assume. If `MonoTools` uses idealised 27 d sectors, that is the single
strongest utility argument available for the paper.

## 8. Performance

Measured on a 4-physical-core i5-1038NG7, `codeastro` conda env.

- **~1,400 systems/s** on a log-spaced 20–500 d grid.
- Cost tracks the **alias count**, not the transit count: it peaks near 30–45 d
  (~1,000 µs/system, ~30 aliases) and falls to ~300 µs/system by 500 d, where
  most systems have fewer than two transits. A grid restricted to >100 d runs
  ~2.5× faster per system.
- Linear in each of periods × phases × sky positions independently.
- **254 bytes/row** in memory, 159 bytes/row pickled.
- Scoring: ~0.5× a baseline grid pass per strategy.
- `run_grid`'s period loop is embarrassingly parallel; not yet parallelized.

**A sector number does not tell you where it pointed.** See decision D11.

### Flyability — not yet enforced

None of the five strategies has been checked against the spacecraft's actual
pointing constraints. They are currently *sector relabellings*, not schedules.
From the TESS Observatory Guide and the extended-mission planning guide:

- **Anti-solar constraint (binding).** The combined FOV centre must sit ideally
  within **15°**, and no more than **30°**, of the antisolar direction in
  ecliptic longitude. This is a *power* constraint — solar-panel illumination.
- **Sunshade / thermal.** Holding one field for more than **two spacecraft
  orbits (~42 d)** is hard. This is exactly what the four-orbit sectors 97/98
  relax, by pushing toward the ±30° limit.
- **Nominal cadence.** ~28° eastward in ecliptic longitude per lunar month.
- **Earth and Moon** crossings scatter light over ~120° in ecliptic latitude;
  avoidable by timing, not by pointing.

The anti-solar test is a **hard filter that can be applied mechanically**: for
each synthesized window, compute the antisolar ecliptic longitude at that date
and require the reused pointing's longitude to fall within 30°. Applying it is
the difference between "here are some windows" and "here is a flyable
schedule", and is the first thing a TESS-team referee will check. **All five
current strategies are at risk**, because each assigns a fixed set of ecliptic
longitudes to future dates chosen without any reference to where the Sun will
be. Which of them actually violate the constraint, and by how much, is an open
empirical question — the filter has not been run. **Not yet implemented; see
§10.**

### Two naming/scoping opportunities

- **Bouma et al. 2017's vocabulary is standard**: `pole` (stare at an ecliptic
  pole for ≥1 yr), `hemi` (repeat the primary mission on one hemisphere),
  `all-sky` (both hemispheres in one year at ~14 d/sector). `repeat_y1_south`
  and `repeat_y2_north` are `hemi` variants; there is no `pole` strategy here
  and it is the obvious missing one, since a pole stare maximises baseline per
  star and is the natural upper limit on alias resolution.
- **Cycle 9 is real, not hypothetical.** Sectors 108–121 (2026-09-07 →
  2027-09-19) are public and already encoded in tess-point 0.9.3 (agreeing with
  MIT's table to <0.012°, D10). The *actual* proposed schedule can therefore be
  scored alongside the hypotheticals, which turns the paper from a methods note
  into an input to a live decision.

## 10. Open questions

- **The revisit-gap experiment does not yet exist.** Goal 2 asks how to *stagger*
  returns to a field, but every current strategy holds timing fixed and varies
  only sky selection (§3). The needed experiment is the transpose: fix the
  pointing set, sweep the revisit gap `dt`, and measure surviving aliases as a
  function of `dt` and period. The physics says this should be strongly
  structured rather than monotonic — the ladder `P = dt/n` means a gap of `dt`
  leaves aliases spaced `dt/n(n+1)` apart, so which periods stay confused depends
  on the *arithmetic* relationship between the gap and the period, not just on
  the gap's length. A schedule of two revisits at gaps sharing few common
  divisors should outperform two at similar gaps. **That structure — if it holds
  — is the paper's actual recommendation**, and none of the five current
  strategies probes it. Highest-priority item in this list.
- **Rank by systems or by stars?** `n_upgraded` and `stars_upgraded` disagree
  on ordering. Which is "the" answer is a science decision.
- **Cost matching.** Comparing a 13-sector strategy against the union of two
  13-sector strategies is not a fair comparison. Any union figure needs a
  matched budget.
- **Do predicted windows belong in a published number?** The `predicted` flag
  exists to let them be excluded; no decision yet.
- **Prior on period.** The current grid is uniform in log P with uniform phase.
  A yield forecast would need to weight by an occurrence rate — but that is a
  *downstream* user's choice, and keeping the grid unweighted is what makes the
  output reusable as a factor (D16). Flat is likely the right answer here; the
  open part is only whether to *also* publish an occurrence-weighted version.
- **Parallelization** — deferred, not needed at current grid sizes.

**Settled, not open:** detectability, stellar parameters and transit depths are
out of scope by design (D16), not pending work. They belong to the second and
third factors of the yield decomposition and would obscure the schedule
dependence this project exists to isolate.

Raised by the literature review (2026-08-21) and **not yet decided**:

- **Promote `n_surviving` to a primary metric — probably the primary one.**
  Goal 2 is stated as minimising *surviving aliases per period range*, because
  that is what sets the follow-up cost. But every metric currently reported
  (`n_upgraded`, `stars_upgraded`, `transition`) is built on the **flag**, which
  is a coarse quantisation of exactly that quantity: a system going from 38
  surviving aliases to 4 is an enormous reduction in follow-up burden and
  registers as *no change at all*, since it stays `AMBIGUOUS` throughout. For
  scale, Cooke et al. 2021 report an average of 38 aliases per duotransit
  system, so there is a lot of room inside that one flag value.

  `n_surviving` and `n_surviving_new` are already computed and carried through
  scoring — the data is there, only the summary statistics ignore it. Reporting
  the distribution of `n_surviving_new / n_surviving` by period bin looks like
  the most direct expression of the actual objective, and is cheap. **This may
  be the most consequential unmade decision in this list**, since it changes
  what the strategy ranking measures rather than adding a footnote to it.

  Expected information gain in bits (Loredo et al. 2011; Dzigan & Zucker 2011)
  is then the natural formalisation: under a flat prior over a discrete alias
  set the entropy is just `log(n_surviving)`, so the reduction is analytic and
  the objective becomes a number of bits of period information bought per unit
  of telescope time. *Argument against the bits framing specifically:* alias
  counts are directly interpretable as "how many nights of follow-up", which is
  the currency the recommendation is denominated in, and taking a log may
  obscure that for no gain.
- **Implement the anti-solar feasibility filter** (§9). Mechanical, and probably
  the single highest-value addition for credibility — but it may invalidate
  strategies already scored, so it should land before R2 rather than after.
- **Score the real Cycle 9 schedule** (sectors 108–121) alongside the
  hypotheticals.
- **Adopt Bouma et al. 2017's `pole`/`hemi`/`all-sky` names**, and add a `pole`
  strategy, which is currently missing and is the natural limiting case.
- **A probabilistic version of `AMBIGUOUS`?** Baluev 2012 (MNRAS 422, 2372)
  gives a published test — Vuong's closeness test — for whether two rival period
  models are yet observationally distinguishable. That is the statistical
  counterpart of the hard rule-out used here. Relevant only if the binary
  survives/dies criterion turns out to be too brittle at the `PERIOD_MATCH_TOL`
  boundary.
- **The duration→period inversion** (Osborn et al. 2016, NAMASTE; and the 2026
  PINN paper, arXiv:2608.01101) constrains period from transit duration and
  stellar density. It is complementary to combinatorial rule-out, and a referee
  will ask why it is unused. The honest answer — it is a *prior on `n`*, not a
  rule-out, and it is degenerate with eccentricity — needs one paragraph in the
  paper whether or not it is implemented.
- **Verify the sector 97/98 rationale from a primary source.** The literature
  review found — via a search-engine summary, **not** a direct read — that the
  four-orbit sectors exist because relaxing the anti-solar constraint toward ±30°
  permits ~54 d pointings, and that the stated science motivation is the
  long-period one: with periods approaching a sector length, few transits are
  recorded and the next visit comes after a long gap, so "the orbital period will
  not be well defined, making follow-up difficult." **If the primary source says
  this, this project is an input to an active mission decision and the
  introduction should say so in its first paragraph.** Confirm in
  `https://heasarc.gsfc.nasa.gov/docs/tess/docs/Guide-to-TESS-for-EM-planning.pdf`;
  cross-check TESS Data Release Notes for Sector 97 (DRN132) and the 2025
  Astrophysics Senior Review TESS submission. Highest-value open item in this
  list. **Do not cite it until read directly.**
- **Group testing / separating systems.** Each candidate window is a test that
  partitions the alias set into "predicted to transit" and "not"; the goal is a
  minimal set of tests separating all aliases. A dedicated search found **no**
  application of the covering-system, Golomb-ruler or group-testing literature to
  transit period disambiguation — the connection appears genuinely unmade. That
  is either a nice contribution or a sign it is harder than it looks, since the
  tests are not freely choosable (the spacecraft constrains them). Worth one
  framing paragraph; **do not build the paper on it.**

## 11. Revisit geometry — the timing experiment (pilot)

Goal 2 needs an experiment that varies *when* we return, but every strategy in
§9 holds timing fixed (§3). This is that missing experiment, deliberately
stripped of sky: one system, two transits separated by `dt`, and one or more
future windows placed at offset `x·dt`. No footprints, no catalogue, no real
schedule. Code in `experiments/revisit_geometry.py`.

### The signature formulation

The key simplification. Define an alias's **signature** as the set of transit
times it predicts to be *fully detectable* inside the candidate windows. An
alias survives iff its signature exactly equals the true alias's signature:

- an entry the truth lacks → it predicted a transit that was not seen (rule-out a)
- an entry it lacks → it failed to reproduce an observed transit (rule-out b)

So a candidate window does nothing except **partition the ladder into
equivalence classes**, and if the truth lies in class `c` then exactly `|c|`
aliases survive. Under a flat prior over the ladder, with no loop over which
alias is true:

```
E[surviving] = Σ_c |c|² / N        P(solved) = #{c : |c| = 1} / N
E[bits left] = Σ_c |c| log₂|c| / N
```

Adding a window refines the partition, so "how many more observations are
needed" becomes the number of windows required to drive every class to size 1 —
computed exactly rather than simulated. This is where the group-testing framing
(§10) stops being an analogy: each window *is* a test, and the alias set *is*
being separated.

### The zero-information theorem

> If the revisit window has the **same width** `W` as the discovery window and
> is **centred on an integer multiple of `dt`**, then no alias is eliminated —
> for any `dt`, and any number of aliases.

*Proof.* (i) Every alias `dt/n` divides `k·dt` exactly, so all predict a transit
at the centre. (ii) A second transit inside the window requires period
`< W/2` (less half a duration); but surviving the discovery window requires
period `> W/2` (less half a duration), since otherwise a second transit would
have appeared there. The conditions are complementary, so no alias can produce
a distinguishing second transit, and all signatures are identical. ∎

Verified numerically at `dt` = 365, 730, 1460 d and `k` = 2, 3: every case gives
exactly one class. The same arithmetic that *sets* the alias floor (D7) is what
*guarantees* the degeneracy — they are the same inequality with the sign
flipped.

This is the naive schedule: point at the time the longest-period alias predicts
a transit. It re-detects the planet, confirms it is real, and eliminates
nothing.

### Pilot results

Fiducial: `dt` = 730 d (biennial duotransit), 27 d windows, 54 viable aliases.
The 13.5 d shortest alias is *emergent* from the 27 d discovery window, not
imposed — which is a cleaner justification for D7's floor than the BLS argument.

| placement `x` | E[surviving] | P(solved) | bits left |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 (centred one gap later) | **54.0** | 0% | 5.75 |
| 1/2 | 17.4 | 24% | 3.36 |
| 1/3 | 9.1 | 44% | 2.19 |
| 1/4 | 7.3 | 50% | 1.88 |
| 0.618 (golden ratio) | 4.2 | 48% | 1.32 |
| best found (≈0.268) | **3.3** | 52% | — |

**A factor of 16 in follow-up burden, for the same 27 days of telescope time.**
The structure is arithmetic, not monotonic in `dt`: `figures/revisit_sweep.png`
shows sharp spikes at every low-order rational, with height ordered by
denominator, on a background of ~4.5. Two windows show the same effect in the
*difference* `x₂ − x₁` (`figures/revisit_two_windows.png`) — a second window
placed a simple rational away from the first largely re-asks its question.

Window *width* matters more than waiting longer
(`figures/revisit_width.png`): at the degenerate placement, widening the revisit
window from 27 → 54 d takes E[surviving] from 54 to 14, because only a window
wider than the discovery window can fit a second transit of the shortest alias.
**This is exactly what the four-orbit sectors 97/98 do**, and it is an
independent, quantitative argument for that design choice — worth checking
against the mission's own stated rationale (§10).

### Not yet included

Phase of the transit within the discovery window is fixed at centre;
`min_period` is emergent rather than swept; per-alias durations are used here
whereas `tess_window.aliases` passes the *true* period's duration to every alias
(a discrepancy to reconcile before any of this feeds the main pipeline); and the
prior over the ladder is flat. None of these should move the spike structure,
but all should be marginalised over before the result is published.

### D1 · 2026-08-20 · Explicit orbit-window table, not midtime ± 13.5 d
Sector spans measured at 24.3–57.4 d with 2–8 segments. Any uniform-sector
approximation misassigns transits, particularly post-sector-56.

### D2 · 2026-08-20 · A transit must fall entirely inside one window
`tc ± T/2` must fit. Rejects events straddling downlink gaps and sector edges.
Conservative and physically motivated; the alternative (midpoint-in-window)
would credit transits that were only partly observed.

### D3 · 2026-08-20 · Future sectors synthesized from the recent duty cycle
Median cadence, span, segment count and gap of the last 20 real sectors, so
strategies inherit current behaviour rather than an idealised 27 d block. All
strategies share timing, isolating sky selection as the only variable.

### D4 · 2026-08-20 · Period grid 20–500 d, log-spaced; phase uniform on [0,1)
Below ~20 d a standard search succeeds anyway; 500 d is roughly the point where
recovery becomes negligible over a 2,937 d baseline. Log spacing matches how
occurrence rates are reported.

### D5 · 2026-08-20 · Collapse targets onto distinct sector combinations
2,200 combinations stand in for 7,446 multi-sector targets. `weight` carries the
star count so sky-averaged completeness stays meaningful. Runs use
`min_sectors=2`; single-sector targets are closed-form.

### D6 · 2026-08-20 · Duration scales as P^(1/3) at solar density
`T = 13 hr · (P/365)^(1/3)`, central transit. Matters because a 13 hr event is
a non-trivial fraction of a 6.6 d orbit segment near its edges.

### D7 · 2026-08-20 · Alias floor at 13 d
Periods shorter than this would have been found by an ordinary BLS search, so
they are not credible aliases for a duo-transit candidate.

*Amended 2026-08-21 — the justification, not the value.* The BLS argument is
weaker than it looked. A large fraction of real long-period TESS detections did
**not** come from a folded periodogram search at all: Planet Hunters TESS has
surfaced 90 candidates, **73 of them single transits**, via visual inspection and
CNNs on non-phase-folded data (Eisner et al. 2020/2021; AJ 2025,
doi:10.3847/1538-3881/add46d). So "BLS would have found it" is not a reliable
statement about what searches can find. The floor is retained at 13 d and
rejustified as a **definitional convention** — it bounds the alias ladder and
defines the period regime this study is about — rather than as a claim about
detectability. Cooke et al. 2021 use ~10 d from a different argument entirely
("only one transit per observing run"); the value is kept at 13 d for continuity
with R1-era code, but the discrepancy must be declared wherever an ambiguous
fraction is compared with theirs, and a sensitivity check at 10 d would cost one
batch run.

### D8 · 2026-08-21 · `max_sector="auto"` via `last_complete_sector()`
Never hardcode the last usable sector. Sectors still observing have truncated
segment lists and would look like real coverage with a fabricated gap.

### D9 · 2026-08-21 · Encode the sector 102/103 orbit-table bug as data
`ORBIT_TABLE_ERRATA` applies only while the defect signature matches, so it
no-ops once MIT republishes. Chosen over editing the CSV so the correction is
visible, tested, and self-retiring. Reported upstream.

### D10 · 2026-08-21 · Do **not** rebuild the `target_df*.csv` catalogs
Verified three ways: tess-point 0.9.3's RA/Dec/roll matches MIT's authoritative
`Years1-8_pointings_long.csv` to 0.000° for sectors 1–107 (<0.012° for
108–121); a fresh `tess_stars2px` rebuild reproduced the cached CSVs with zero
differences across 8,290 targets / 54,024 star-sectors; and the Year 8 40° roll
shows up in the coverage (median |ec_lat| 53.8° → 41.2°). Only the *windows* go
stale, not the sky coverage.
*Caveat recorded at the time:* checking a cached CSV against the installed
`tess_stars2px` only proves reproducibility. Correctness requires comparing
tess-point's pointing table against MIT's CSV — same-version agreement is
guaranteed and meaningless.

### D11 · 2026-08-21 · Renamed `ecliptic_y5` → `y5_mixed_ns`; added a real `ecliptic`
Sectors 56–68 are not ecliptic. Per-sector median ecliptic latitude is +53…+59
for 56–60 then −47…−57 for 61–68 — a lopsided north-then-south mix that never
approaches the plane. It had been winning the strategy comparison for a reason
the name actively concealed. The genuinely ecliptic sectors (median |ec_lat| =
6°) are 42–46, 70–72, 91–92; only ten exist, spanning ecliptic longitude
0–190° only, and they reach 1,403 multi-sector targets against ~3,400–3,800 for
the hemisphere strategies.

### D12 · 2026-08-21 · Strategy scoring re-analyses rather than pruning
The previous implementation incrementally pruned each system's stored alias
ladder. It was ~2× cheaper but had two structural flaws: pruning only removes
candidate periods, so it could score **only** `ambiguous → solved` and was blind
to mono and missed systems; and it compared aliases at *window* granularity, so
two periods placing a transit anywhere in the same window looked identical even
when predicted times differed by days. On a 6,720-system test grid the honest
re-analysis found **445 upgrades where pruning found 160** — the
`mono → ambiguous` channel alone (161) matched the entire old headline. Since
mono (22.9%) and missed (27.9%) together dwarf ambiguous (20.3%), this changes
what the study measures. `aliases.update_with_windows` was deleted rather than
kept alongside, to avoid two divergent implementations.

### D13 · 2026-08-21 · Report both `n_upgraded` and `stars_upgraded`
They rank strategies differently. Recorded as an open question rather than
silently resolved by the sort order of a CSV.

### D14 · 2026-08-21 · Adopt community terminology; declare `AMBIGUOUS` a superset
Use monotransit / duotransit / biennial duotransit / period alias, and introduce
`SOLVED` as "period-resolved" (`LITERATURE_REVIEW.md` §2). The four-state
*ordered outcome* scheme has no precedent — the literature classifies
*candidates*, not survey outcomes — so the states get defined early and mapped
explicitly, with `NO_TRANSIT` flagged as having no counterpart because existing
studies condition on detection.

The consequential half: **`AMBIGUOUS` is broader than "duotransit"**, since it
admits ≥3-transit systems with surviving aliases. Renaming was considered and
rejected — the name describes the epistemic state correctly, which is the point
of the scheme — so the superset relation is declared instead, and any comparison
against a published duo count must first restrict to `n_observed == 2`. Without
this, the 20.3% ambiguous fraction reads as comparable to Hawthorn et al. 2024's
85 duotransit candidates and is not.

### D15 · 2026-08-21 · Position against Cooke et al. 2021 as the method's origin
Both rule-out criteria in §5 are published in Cooke et al. 2021 (MNRAS 500,
5088), nearly verbatim. The introduction cites them as the origin of the
technique and claims only three extensions: the whole real 8-year baseline
rather than a Year-1/Year-3 pair; the four-state outcome algebra applied to
every sky position rather than to a detected candidate list; and the pointing
schedule as the decision variable. Claiming the alias ladder as novel would be
both wrong and the fastest available route to a hostile referee report.

Recorded because it constrains how results are framed, not just how they are
cited: the novelty claim must be pitched at *schedule-as-decision-variable*,
since Cooke et al. do already use TESS coverage itself as a rule-out mechanism.

### D16 · 2026-08-21 · Compute one factor of the yield, not the yield
Scope decision, taken deliberately rather than forced by effort. A duo-transit
yield needs stellar parameters, population-level planet distributions,
photometric precision, detectability and vetting efficiency; each is its own
research programme, and carrying all of them makes the schedule-dependent
effect — the only factor the mission can *choose* — impossible to examine on its
own. This project therefore computes the first term of
`yield = window function × detectability × astrophysics` exactly, and stops
there.

Two consequences, both deliberate:
- **Reusability is a design goal.** Results are reported resolved by period and
  sky position so others can supply the remaining factors and multiply through.
  A cleanly separated first factor is more useful than another end-to-end yield
  entangled with one group's stellar assumptions.
- **Outcome fractions are never yields.** Rodel et al. 2024 put giant-planet
  completeness below 10% at 200–400 d, so the second factor is small and
  strongly period-dependent in this exact regime. An unqualified "28.9% solved"
  will be misread, so every reported fraction is labelled a necessary condition.

Not a limitation awaiting repair: adding an SNR model would answer a different
question at the cost of this one.
